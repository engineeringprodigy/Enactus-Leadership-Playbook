01

The RACI Matrix

RACI is a decision-rights tool that eliminates the two most common ownership failures: nobody knows who owns something, or everybody thinks they do. One clear matrix, zero ambiguity.

The core idea: For every key task or decision your team makes, assign exactly one letter to each person — R, A, C, or I. If two people share an R, you have a problem. If nobody has an A, you have a bigger problem. The RACI forces clarity before the work starts, not after the blame starts.
🕳️
The gap problem
Two people both assume the other is handling something. Nobody does it. The task falls through the cracks entirely — and both blame each other.
🪢
The overlap problem
Two people both think they own the same decision. They pull in different directions, create confusion, and waste energy on internal conflict instead of the actual work.
🗂️
The RACI solution
Before work begins, map every decision to one owner. Everyone else's role is defined too — consulted, informed, or simply not involved. No gaps, no overlaps.
R
R
Responsible
The person who does the work. They execute the task and are responsible for getting it done. There can be more than one R, but keep it tight — the more Rs, the less accountability.
"Who is actually doing this?"
A
A
Accountable
The one person who owns the outcome. They answer for the result — good or bad. There must be exactly one A per task. Never zero. Never two. The A approves the final output.
"Who owns this when it's done — or not done?"
C
C
Consulted
People whose input is needed before a decision is made. They have expertise or a stake in the outcome. Two-way communication — they give input, the R/A considers it.
"Who needs to weigh in before we proceed?"
I
I
Informed
People who need to know what happened — but after the fact. One-way communication only. They don't block or shape decisions. They just need to be kept in the loop.
"Who needs to know this happened, but doesn't need a say?"
The golden rules
One A per task. Always.
If two people are accountable for the same outcome, nobody is. Shared accountability is just accountability without consequence. Pick one person and hold them to it.
Limit your Rs
Having five people responsible for a task is the same as having none. If your team genuinely needs multiple Rs, split the task into subtasks and give each one its own owner.
Don't over-consult
Too many Cs slow everything down. Reserve C for people whose input will genuinely change the output. If they're just being looped in as a courtesy, make them an I.
Build it before the project starts
A RACI filled out after conflict has already started is just a post-mortem. Its value is in setting expectations upfront — before anyone assumes anything.
Example — Enactus project RACI
Task / Decision Team Lead Project Manager Outreach Lead Marketing Lead Finance Lead
Set semester OKRs A R C C I
Plan community event A C R C I
Approve new partnership R I A I C
Publish social content I I C A I
Approve budget spend A C I I R
Onboard new members C A I I I
Weekly team check-in A R I I I
R Responsible — does the work  ·  A Accountable — owns the outcome  ·  C Consulted — input needed before  ·  I Informed — kept in the loop after
How to build yours in 4 steps
01
List your key tasks
Write down the 8–12 most important recurring tasks or decisions your team makes. Don't list everything — just the ones where ownership confusion has caused problems before.
02
List your team members
Put each person's name or role across the top of the matrix. Keep it to the people who are genuinely involved — not everyone on the team for every task.
03
Assign letters together
Fill it in as a team, not alone. Disagreements during this process are valuable — they surface assumptions that would otherwise cause conflict mid-project.
04
Share and revisit
Post the RACI where everyone can see it. Revisit at the start of each semester or whenever a new project or role is added. A stale RACI is almost as bad as none.
If everyone is accountable, nobody is.
The RACI isn't bureaucracy — it's respect. It respects people's time by telling them exactly where they're needed and where they're not.
02

Ownership-Focused
1:1s

Most 1:1s are just status updates in disguise. Ownership-focused 1:1s are different — they're conversations that actively build someone's sense of agency, confidence, and accountability over time.

The shift: Instead of asking "what have you been working on?" — which pulls for tasks — ask "what did you decide this week?" and "what are you proud of owning?" These questions reframe the conversation around agency, not activity. Over time, they rewire how your team members see their role.
✕   Status-update 1:1s
  • Leader does most of the talking
  • Conversation centres on tasks completed
  • Problems are brought to the leader to solve
  • Member feels managed, not developed
  • Nothing changes week to week
✓   Ownership-focused 1:1s
  • Member does most of the talking
  • Conversation centres on decisions made and problems solved
  • Problems are brought with proposed solutions
  • Member feels trusted and challenged
  • Member grows visibly over the semester
The two ownership questions
⭐ Primary question — ask every single 1:1
"What did you decide this week — and what did you consider before deciding?"
This question does two things at once. It tells you whether they're actually making decisions (not just executing tasks), and it surfaces their reasoning so you can coach their judgment — not just their output. If they can't name a decision, that's information too.
"
⭐ Secondary question
"What are you proud of owning this week?"
Invites them to identify their own wins and reinforce a sense of ownership identity. Over time, this builds the habit of seeing themselves as an owner, not a task-executor.
For development
"Where did you feel stuck waiting for someone else's approval?"
Surfaces bottlenecks in your team's decision-making culture. If they're always waiting on you, you may be accidentally creating dependency.
For growth
"What would you do differently if you had full authority over this?"
This unlocks their thinking beyond their current scope. It often reveals ideas worth implementing — and it builds the muscles of strategic thinking.
For accountability
"What commitment did you make last week, and did you keep it?"
Quietly holds people to their word without confrontation. Starting each 1:1 this way creates a culture where follow-through is the norm, not the exception.
For initiative
"What problem did you solve before it became my problem?"
Actively rewards proactive behaviour. When you celebrate this in 1:1s, you get more of it. What gets recognised gets repeated.
A 30-minute ownership 1:1 structure
30-minute template TEMPLATE
0–2 min
Check-in
One quick question to open the space. Not about work — about them.
  • "How are you actually doing this week — outside of work?"
2–10 min
Ownership review
The core of the meeting. Let them drive — you listen and ask follow-ups.
  • "What did you decide this week? What did you consider?"
  • "What are you proud of owning?"
  • "What commitment from last week did you keep?"
10–20 min
Blockers and growth
What's in their way? Where are they waiting unnecessarily? What do they want to own more of?
  • "Where did you feel stuck waiting for approval?"
  • "What would you do differently with full authority?"
  • "What do you want to take more ownership of next month?"
20–28 min
Your input
Now you speak. Offer feedback, remove blockers, share context they need, and make any decisions that only you can make.
28–30 min
Commitment
Each person names one thing they will do before the next 1:1. Write it down. Open with it next time.
  • "What's the one thing you're committing to before we meet again?"
The best 1:1s make the leader talk less and the team member think more.
Your job in a 1:1 isn't to report or instruct — it's to coach. Ask more than you tell. Listen more than you speak. The answers reveal more than the updates ever will.
03

The "Solve It
and Tell Me" Rule

Most teams default to asking for permission. The Solve It and Tell Me rule flips this — it tells your team to fix problems first, then report back. It builds a culture of action over approval-seeking.

The rule in one line: "If you encounter a problem within your scope, solve it — then tell me what you did and why." This one sentence, applied consistently, transforms a team of task-executors into a team of decision-makers.
The autonomy spectrum — where does your team sit?
Level 1
Wait to be told
Level 2
Ask for permission
Level 3
Recommend, then act
Level 4
Act, then report
Level 5
Fully autonomous
← Dependent The "Solve It" rule targets Level 4 Fully owned →
🔧
How it works
When a team member encounters a problem within their area of responsibility, they don't bring it to you to solve. They solve it — using their best judgment — and then tell you what they did, what they decided, and why. You respond with feedback, not a fix.
"I noticed our event venue fell through. I contacted three alternatives, confirmed one for the same date and price, and notified the attendees. Just wanted to loop you in."
🧠
Why it works
Ownership is a muscle. Every time someone waits for approval on a problem they could have solved, that muscle weakens. Every time they act decisively and it works out, it strengthens. The Solve It rule creates hundreds of small reps each semester that compound into a genuinely autonomous team.
Leaders who need to approve everything become the bottleneck. Leaders who trust and debrief become multipliers.
How to implement it
01
Define the scope
Be explicit about what decisions each person can make alone vs. what requires escalation. Use your RACI as the boundary map. "Solve It" applies within their A and R columns — not beyond them.
02
Announce the rule clearly
Don't let people discover it by accident. Explicitly tell your team: "From now on, when you hit a problem in your lane — solve it, then tell me. I trust your judgment. I want to hear what you did, not ask you what to do."
03
Celebrate the first win
The first time someone solves a problem and tells you after, make a big deal of it in the team. Name it. Celebrate it. This signals to everyone else that this behaviour is rewarded, not just tolerated.
04
Debrief, don't criticise
When someone solves something imperfectly, debrief with curiosity — not judgment. "What led you to that decision? What would you consider next time?" Punishment for imperfect decisions trains people to stop deciding.
05
Stop answering solvable questions
When someone brings you a problem they could solve themselves, don't solve it. Ask: "What do you think we should do?" Then: "Great — go do that and tell me how it turns out." Redirect consistently.
06
Expand scope over time
Start with small, low-risk decisions. As people demonstrate good judgment, gradually expand the scope of what they can solve independently. Trust is earned in increments — and given in the same way.
Before and after — real scenarios
Same situation. Two cultures.
✕ Before — permission culture
"Hey, the printer at our event venue is broken and we can't print the sign-in sheets. What should we do?"
✓ After — solve it culture
"The printer broke so I sent the sign-in sheet to my phone, set up a QR code at the door, and texted three volunteers to bring their laptops as backup. We're covered."
✕ Before — permission culture
"One of our community partners said they can't make the meeting tomorrow. Should I reschedule the whole thing or keep going without them?"
✓ After — solve it culture
"Our partner dropped out so I moved their agenda item to a follow-up call and kept the meeting as planned. I've already sent them a summary invite for next Thursday."
✕ Before — permission culture
"We're running low on budget for the event. Should I cut the catering or ask for more money?"
✓ After — solve it culture
"We were over budget so I renegotiated the catering to a lighter option, saving $180, and reallocated it to the AV setup which was the bigger priority. Finance is updated."
Stop asking for permission. Start reporting results.
The most empowering thing a leader can say is: "I trust your judgment. Solve it and tell me what you did." Say it often enough, and eventually — they will.
