Building
Clarity

Clarity is the foundation of every high-performing team. Without it, even the most talented people waste time guessing at goals, stepping on each other's toes, and repeating the same mistakes. This page shows you exactly how to build it.

How to Set
Clear OKRs

OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) turn vague ambitions into measurable outcomes. They answer two questions: where are we going? and how will we know we got there?

The rule: Objectives are qualitative and inspiring — they tell the team what to aim for and why it matters. Key Results are quantitative and binary — at the end of the period, you either hit the number or you didn't. If you can't measure it, it's not a Key Result.
O — Objective
The destination
Inspiring, qualitative, time-bound. Answers "what do we want to achieve and why does it matter?"
+
KR — Key Results (2–5)
The milestones
Specific, numeric, measurable. Answers "how will we know we've arrived?" Each KR is a clear number to hit.
=
Result
Focused progress
Everyone knows the goal, owns their piece, and can see if the team is winning or not — in real time.
1
Pick your Objective
Choose 1–3 objectives per quarter. Each should be ambitious but achievable. Ask: "If we only accomplished this one thing, would it be a great quarter?"
2
Write 2–5 Key Results
For each objective, write 2–5 measurable outcomes. Every KR must have a number. If you can't attach a metric, turn it into a milestone with a binary yes/no.
3
Assign an owner
Every KR needs a single person accountable for it — not a team. That person tracks the number, surfaces blockers, and reports progress weekly.
4
Share publicly
OKRs only work if the whole team can see them. Post them somewhere visible. Transparency creates alignment and peer accountability.
5
Review weekly
Do a 10-minute OKR check-in each week. Each owner gives a confidence score (0–100%) on hitting their KR. Drop confidence = early warning signal.
6
Grade at the end
At the end of the quarter, score each KR 0–1.0. A 0.7 is a good score — if you're consistently hitting 1.0, your targets weren't ambitious enough.
Real example — Enactus project team, Q3
Build a community launch for our social enterprise that generates real impact and public visibility
  • Launch the pilot program with at least 30 active community participants by September 30
  • Secure 2 local business or sponsor partnerships contributing $500+ each in resources or funding
  • Achieve an 80%+ satisfaction score from participants in the post-program survey
  • Generate 3 pieces of media coverage (news, social, podcast) documenting the launch
  • Present impact report with measurable outcomes at the national showcase
✕  Weak OKRs — don't do this
  • Objective: "Improve our team's performance"
  • KR: "Work harder on outreach this quarter"
  • KR: "Get more people involved in the project"
  • KR: "Try to post more on social media"
✓  Strong OKRs — do this instead
  • Objective: "Make our community program impossible to ignore by December"
  • KR: "Onboard 50 active participants with 70%+ retention"
  • KR: "Run 4 events with avg. 25 attendees each"
  • KR: "Hit 500 newsletter subscribers from zero"
Common mistakes
Too many OKRs
More than 3 objectives per quarter means nothing is truly a priority. Everything important can't be the most important thing.
Activity-based KRs
"Run 10 meetings" is an activity, not a result. KRs measure outcomes, not effort. Ask: "What changes in the world if we do this?"
Set-and-forget
OKRs with no weekly check-in become wallpaper. The magic is in the regular review — that's what keeps teams honest and on track.
Playing it safe
If your team hits 100% of KRs every quarter, your targets are too easy. Good OKRs should feel slightly uncomfortable to commit to.
Clarity is not a document. It's a habit.
Setting OKRs once and never reviewing them is worse than not having them — it breeds cynicism. Make them a living tool, not a filing exercise.

How to Clarify
Team Roles

Role confusion is one of the most common hidden killers of team performance. When people don't know what they own — or who owns what — tasks fall through the cracks, effort is duplicated, and conflict brews.

The fix: For every seat on your team, create a one-page Role Charter. It should fit on a single page, take under 30 minutes to write, and be visible to everyone. If your team can't describe each other's roles in one sentence, you have a clarity problem.
📋
Write the charter
For each role, document: the mission of the role in one sentence, the 3–5 core responsibilities, what decisions they can make alone, and who they report to and collaborate with.
🤝
Co-create with the person
Don't write role charters alone and hand them down. Sit with the person who holds the role and build it together — they'll know things you don't, and they'll own it more if they wrote it.
🔄
Review every semester
Teams evolve. Roles shift. Block 30 minutes each semester to review every role charter together. Outdated charters are almost as bad as no charters at all.
Role Charter — Example TEMPLATE
Role Title
Head of Community Outreach
Mission
Build and sustain the external relationships that make our program visible, trusted, and growing in the community.
Core Responsibilities
  • Own all external partner and community stakeholder relationships
  • Plan and execute at least 2 community engagement events per semester
  • Track outreach metrics and report to the team lead bi-weekly
  • Manage the outreach budget and document all expenses
  • Onboard and brief any external volunteers or guests
Decisions They Own
  • Which partners and organizations to contact or prioritize
  • How outreach events are structured and promoted
  • Content and tone of external communications
Escalate To
Team Lead — for any commitment over $200, new formal partnerships, or conflicts with external stakeholders
Works Closely With
Marketing Lead (content), Project Manager (event logistics), Finance Lead (budget approvals)
Success Looks Like
At least 3 active community partnerships this semester, 2 events run with 80%+ attendance, and outreach KRs hit on time.
Bonus tool — The RACI matrix

When multiple people are involved in a task or decision, use a RACI to remove ambiguity about who does what.

Task / Decision Team Lead Project Mgr Outreach Lead Marketing Lead
Set semester OKRs A R C C
Plan community event A C R C
Approve new partnership R I A I
Publish social content I I C R
Weekly team check-in A R I I
R = Responsible (does the work)  ·  A = Accountable (owns the outcome)  ·  C = Consulted (input needed)  ·  I = Informed (kept in the loop)
If two people think they own the same thing, nobody owns it.
Every task, project, and decision needs exactly one accountable person. Not a team. Not "we." One person.

How to Document
Simple SOPs

A Standard Operating Procedure (SOP) is a step-by-step document that explains how to complete a recurring task — so that anyone on the team can do it, not just the person who figured it out first.

The rule of thumb: If you've done a task more than once and you'll do it again — document it. The goal isn't a perfect manual. It's a good-enough guide that saves your future self (and your teammates) from starting from scratch every single time.
Without SOPs
  • Knowledge lives in one person's head
  • Every handover is a game of telephone
  • New members take weeks to get up to speed
  • The same mistakes repeat every semester
  • The team can't scale — it depends on one expert
With SOPs
  • Anyone can pick up any task without asking
  • Onboarding takes days, not weeks
  • Institutional knowledge survives team turnover
  • Mistakes get fixed once — in the doc, for everyone
  • The team scales because the system does the teaching
How to create one in 5 steps
01
Pick the task
Choose any task you repeat. Start with the ones that trip people up most or take the longest to explain.
02
Do it once, out loud
Complete the task while narrating every step. Record it or write as you go — don't try to remember it afterward.
03
Write the steps
Turn your narration into a numbered list. Use plain language. One action per step. Add screenshots or examples where helpful.
04
Test it with someone new
Give the SOP to someone who's never done the task. Watch them follow it. Anywhere they get stuck — fix the doc.
SOP Template — Example
SOP: How to onboard a new Enactus team member EXAMPLE
Purpose
Ensure every new member is set up with the right access, context, and connections within their first week — so they can contribute from day one.
Owner
Team Lead or designated Onboarding Lead. Updated by: whoever finds a gap or error.
Trigger
A new member has been confirmed and has accepted their role.
Tools needed
Google Drive Slack / WhatsApp Notion / Docs Calendar invite
Steps
  1. Send a welcome message within 24 hours of their confirmation — introduce yourself and share excitement
  2. Add them to the team Slack/WhatsApp group and tag them in a welcome message
  3. Share access to the team Google Drive folder (add their email directly, don't just send a link)
  4. Send them the Team Handbook or Onboarding Doc — ask them to read sections 1–3 before their first meeting
  5. Schedule a 1:1 intro call within the first 3 days — use this call to cover their role, the team's OKRs, and to answer questions
  6. Pair them with a buddy (an existing team member) for their first 2 weeks
  7. Add them to the team meeting calendar invite and introduce them at the next team call
  8. Check in after week 1: ask how they're settling in and if anything is unclear
Definition of done
The new member has attended their first team meeting, has access to all tools, has completed their 1:1, and can describe their role and the team's current OKRs.

Tips for keeping SOPs alive
Keep them short
A one-page SOP that people actually read beats a 10-page manual no one opens. Aim for under 20 steps for any single task.
Store them in one place
Every SOP goes in the same shared folder — not someone's personal drive, not in a Slack thread. One source of truth, always.
Update when things break
When someone follows an SOP and gets stuck, fix the doc immediately. An SOP that leads people astray is worse than none at all.
Name them clearly
Use plain names like "SOP: How to post on Instagram" not "Social Media Guidelines v2 Final." People should find the doc before they even open it.
Document it once. Save everyone hours, forever.
The best time to write an SOP is while you're doing the task for the second time. The worst time is after the only person who knows how to do it has left the team.
