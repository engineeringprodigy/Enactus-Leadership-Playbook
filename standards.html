Standards don't enforce themselves — they need to be written down, co-created, and embedded into the daily rhythm of the team. This page gives you three practical tools to make quality consistent, self-managing, and impossible to argue with.

01

Clear Acceptance
Criteria on Every Task

Acceptance criteria are the 2–5 specific, testable conditions that define when a task is complete. They turn "I think this is done" into "yes, it passes — here's the evidence."

The core idea: Before a task begins, write down what "done" looks like — in concrete, checkable terms. Anyone — the person doing the work, a peer, or a future reviewer — should be able to read the criteria and say yes or no with no ambiguity. If you can't test it, it's not a criterion.
Input
The task
Any piece of work — deliverable, event, design, document, campaign, or decision — that will be handed off or reviewed.
+
Process
2–5 testable conditions
Written before work starts. Each one is specific, observable, and either passes or fails — no grey area.
=
Output
Self-checked quality
Work that anyone can verify without a manager present. Fewer revisions. Faster sign-off. Consistent output across the team.
How to write acceptance criteria in 4 steps
01
Start with the outcome, not the activity
Ask: "What does success look like from the perspective of the person receiving this work?" Write from that perspective — not from the task-doer's point of view. The outcome is what matters, not the effort.
Bad: "Work on the event promotion." Good: "The event has been promoted across 3 channels with posts live at least 5 days before the date."
02
Make every criterion testable
Each criterion should be answerable with a clear yes or no. If it requires interpretation or judgment to evaluate, rewrite it with a concrete number, threshold, or observable condition. "Good quality" is not a criterion. "Fewer than 2 typos" is.
Bad: "The report looks professional." Good: "The report uses the team template, has no typos, and fits on 2 pages."
03
Write them before work starts
Acceptance criteria written after the work is done are just rationalizations. The discipline is to write them first — before anyone touches the task. This also forces clarity about what the task actually is before time is wasted on the wrong version.
In your task management tool (Notion, Trello, Asana), add an AC field to every card before assigning it. If you can't fill it in, the task isn't ready to be assigned.
04
Keep it to 2–5 criteria
More than 5 and you're writing a spec, not criteria. Focus on the conditions that would make you genuinely confident the work is good. If everything feels equally important, the task is probably too large — break it down.
If you find yourself writing 10 criteria, split the task into two smaller tasks with 3–5 criteria each.
Example — Community event promotion task EXAMPLE
TASK
Promote the Q3 community launch event across all team channels
  • AC1
    Posts are live on Instagram, LinkedIn, and the team newsletter at least 5 days before the event date ✓ Testable — check timestamps on each platform
  • AC2
    All posts include the event date, time, location, and a registration link ✓ Testable — open each post and verify all 4 elements are present
  • AC3
    Instagram post has been approved by the Marketing Lead before going live ✓ Testable — check for approval comment or sign-off in the task
  • AC4
    At least 15 registrations are confirmed within 48 hours of the first post going live ✓ Testable — check registration form responses
  • AC5
    All team members have shared or re-posted at least one piece of promotion content from their personal accounts ✓ Testable — team lead confirms in the group chat
Testable vs. vague — the difference
✕   Vague — not testable
The document looks good and is well-written
The event was promoted sufficiently
The partner is happy with the outcome
The presentation was professional
The task was completed on time
✓   Testable — clear pass/fail
Document uses the team template and has zero spelling errors
Promoted on 3 platforms, 5+ days before the event date
Partner has responded with written confirmation by Friday
Slides use brand colours, max 8 words per slide, no images stretched
Submitted to the shared folder by 5pm on the agreed date
Common mistakes
Writing criteria after the work
Criteria written retrospectively just validate whatever was produced. The whole point is to set the bar before work starts, so the person doing it knows what to aim for.
Using subjective language
"High quality," "looks good," "thorough," and "professional" are not criteria. Every criterion must be verifiable by someone who wasn't involved in creating the work.
Too many criteria
More than 5 criteria usually means the task is too large or you're writing a full spec. Trim to the 2–5 conditions that would genuinely make you confident the work is done well.
Skipping small tasks
Even quick tasks benefit from 2–3 criteria. "Send the email" becomes "email sent by Thursday, includes the agenda, and has been proofread by one other person." Takes 30 seconds to write, saves significant confusion.
If you can't test it, it's not a standard — it's a wish.
Acceptance criteria turn expectations into evidence. The goal isn't to police your team — it's to make quality so clear that they can police themselves.
02

Team-Owned
Definition of Done

A Definition of Done (DoD) is a short, shared checklist that defines what "complete" means for your team's main work types — created by the team, owned by the team, and applied consistently by everyone.

The key distinction from Acceptance Criteria: Acceptance criteria are task-specific — they change with every task. A Definition of Done is team-wide — it applies to every piece of work in a given category. Think of it as your universal quality floor. No work ships below it, ever.
🤝
Co-created = owned
A DoD handed down by a leader gets ignored. One built by the team gets defended. Hold a session to build it together — the conversation itself surfaces misaligned expectations.
🚦
Consistent floor
Without a DoD, quality depends on who does the work. With one, it depends on the checklist. Every person, every time — same minimum bar.
🚀
No bottleneck
When the DoD is clear, anyone can ship work that meets it — no manager sign-off needed. Quality becomes self-managing, not leader-dependent.
How to build your DoD in one session
01
Pick your work types
Identify the 2–4 main categories of work your team produces. For Enactus, this might be events, documents, presentations, and partner communications. You'll build a DoD checklist for each.
02
Run a "what does done mean?" session
Gather the team. For each work type, ask: "If we handed this off tomorrow and felt genuinely confident about it — what would be true?" Write everything down without filtering. You'll get duplicates, misalignments, and surprises. That's the point.
Prompt: "Imagine a piece of work that is just barely not done. What's missing? Now imagine it's definitely done — what's present?" The gap between those two descriptions is your DoD.
03
Trim to 6–12 items
Combine duplicates, remove aspirational items (those belong in acceptance criteria), and cut anything that isn't genuinely universal. You want the list short enough that people remember it — long enough to actually cover quality.
04
Publish it and use it
Put it somewhere everyone can access instantly — the top of your Notion workspace, pinned in Slack, printed at your desk. Refer to it openly in meetings. Make checking it a visible habit, not a secret ritual.
05
Review it each semester
As your team matures, so should your standards. Schedule a 30-minute DoD review each semester. What's missing? What's outdated? What's so obvious now that it doesn't need to be on the list?

03

Self-Managed
Peer Review

Peer review keeps quality high and knowledge distributed — without creating a bottleneck around one manager or approver. Any qualified teammate can approve work, as long as the acceptance criteria and DoD are both met.

The shift: Traditional review flows everything through the leader — creating delays, dependency, and a single point of failure. Self-managed peer review distributes that responsibility across the team. The leader sets the standard. The team enforces it.
How the process flows
✍️
Work created
Owner completes work against the agreed acceptance criteria
☑️
Self-check
Owner runs through the DoD checklist before requesting review
👥
Peer review
A qualified teammate reviews against the AC and DoD — not personal preference
✅
Approved
Peer confirms all criteria are met. Work ships — no manager required
🔁
Revise if needed
If criteria aren't met, owner revises and resubmits — specific gaps only
The rules that make it work
Review against criteria — not preference
Reviewers evaluate whether the acceptance criteria are met, not whether they would have done it differently. Personal taste is not a reason to request changes. Unmet criteria is. This keeps reviews fast and objective.
Self-check first — always
Work should never go for peer review without the owner first checking it against the DoD themselves. Sending work that obviously fails the checklist disrespects the reviewer's time and signals a culture that doesn't take standards seriously.
Any qualified teammate can approve
"Qualified" means they understand the work type and have read the acceptance criteria. It does not mean they hold a specific role. Distributing approval authority across the team removes single-point bottlenecks and builds shared ownership of quality.
Feedback must be specific
If a reviewer rejects work, they must cite which acceptance criterion or DoD item is not met and why. "This doesn't feel right" is not valid feedback. "AC3 isn't met — the registration link goes to the wrong form" is.
Escalate only genuine disagreements
If the owner and reviewer genuinely disagree on whether a criterion is met — not just style preference — escalate to the team lead for a final call. But this should be rare if criteria are well-written.
Keep a record
Leave a written trail: who reviewed, when, and what was confirmed or flagged. This creates accountability, enables learning, and gives the team lead visibility without being in every review loop themselves.
Reviewer checklist — what a good review looks like
Peer review template TEMPLATE
Before you start
  • Read the acceptance criteria for this task before looking at the work
  • Confirm the owner has self-checked against the DoD (ask if unsure)
  • Confirm you understand what the work is supposed to achieve
During review
  • For each acceptance criterion: does this work clearly pass? Yes or no
  • For each DoD item applicable to this work type: is it met?
  • Note any criterion that is borderline — flag it with a specific question, not a rejection
  • Do not flag things that are preference, style, or "how I would have done it"
If you approve
Write a brief confirmation: "Reviewed [date]. All acceptance criteria met. DoD checked. Approved to ship." Leave this on the task card or in the shared doc.
If you request changes
  • List only the criteria or DoD items that are not met — by number or name
  • For each one, state specifically what's missing or incorrect
  • Do not request changes on items that are met but could be "better"
  • Offer to answer questions — don't leave the owner to guess what you mean
Time expectation
Most peer reviews should take 10–20 minutes. If a review is taking longer, the acceptance criteria are probably too vague — fix the criteria, not just the work.
Before and after — what peer review changes
Same team. Two approaches to quality.
✕ Without peer review
"I think the event plan is ready — I'll send it to the team lead and wait to hear back." Three days pass. The lead makes minor changes. The plan ships late.
✓ With peer review
"I've self-checked against the DoD, all 8 items are done. I've asked Jamie to review against the AC — she confirmed by end of day and we shipped."
✕ Without peer review
"The newsletter went out with the wrong event date. Nobody caught it because only one person looked at it before it sent."
✓ With peer review
"During peer review, AC2 flagged that the event date was missing from the subject line. Fixed before send. Zero corrections needed afterward."
✕ Without peer review
"The leader is the only one who approves anything, so everything queues up. We can't move until they respond — and they're handling 6 other things."
✓ With peer review
"Three people on the team are now qualified reviewers. Work gets approved same-day. The leader is in the loop but not the bottleneck."
Quality is a team sport, not a leader's job.
When your team owns the review process — not just the work — standards stop being rules imposed from above and start being values upheld from within. That's when culture actually works.
