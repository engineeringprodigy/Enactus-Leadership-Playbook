How to Send
an Effective Email

01
Vision
Open with the bigger picture — the outcome, goal, or change you're working toward. The reader should immediately understand why this matters before you've asked for anything.
"We're building something that could genuinely change how our team operates this semester — and I think we're close to a breakthrough."
02
Framing
Set the context. Explain where things stand right now — the situation, the opportunity, the moment in time — so the reader understands the stakes and why now is the right time to act.
"Right now, we're at a critical point. We have momentum, but one gap is standing between where we are and where we need to be."
03
Weakness
Be honest about what you're missing without their support. Vulnerability builds trust — it makes the ask feel genuine, not transactional. This is the most underused step.
"Without the right support here, I know we'll fall short. This is genuinely not something I can solve alone."
04
Pedestal
Make them feel seen. Specifically explain why you're asking them — their unique quality, experience, or position. Not flattery — specificity. Generic compliments fall flat.
"You're one of the few people I know who has actually navigated this. Your perspective isn't just helpful — it's the missing piece."
05
Ask
Make a single, clear, specific request. Don't hedge or bury it. The reader should know exactly what you're asking for and what a "yes" looks like. Vague asks get ignored.
"Would you be open to a 20-minute call this week? I'll come prepared so we use your time well."
02

Understanding
Your Team

A
Type A · Engine
The Driver
  • Autonomous and self-directed
  • Highly organized, detail-oriented
  • Owns big problems without micromanagement
  • High standards — raises the bar for everyone
  • Fast output, thinks about the mission beyond work hours
Your role Clear the path. Give them big problems. Don't micromanage — they'll leave if you do. Retain them at all costs: A players want to work with other A players.
B
Type B · Backbone
The Backbone
  • Steady, reliable, consistent performers
  • Completes assigned tasks well
  • Team-oriented — brings people closer together
  • May lack initiative or need some guidance
  • The majority of most teams
Your role Invest in them. Show them the "why" behind the mission so they start to care deeply. Connect them to purpose and they can become A players.
C
Type C · Underperformer
The Passenger
  • Delivers below expectations
  • Requires frequent supervision
  • Drains team morale and resources
  • May result from unclear expectations or poor onboarding
  • Higher maintenance
Your role Diagnose first — it might be your fault. Train before you fire. If they can't be coached to B or A, you must act decisively. They cost the whole team.
Remember: Always train your B and C players before you fire them — it could be the leader's fault for not properly onboarding or leading with clarity. Avoid recruiting 9-to-5ers; you want people who think about the mission outside of work hours. That requires a leader who makes people genuinely care.

How to Build
a Productive Team

C
🎯
Clarity
Define what success looks like
  • Set clear goals: translate strategy into team and individual OKRs
  • Specify outcomes, not activities — measurable results only
  • Clarify roles: publish a one-page role charter for every seat
  • Document any repeated task as an SOP so anyone can follow it
Example: At the start of every project, share a 1-page brief with goal, KPIs, timeline, and deliverable owners.

O
🔑
Ownership
Give authority with accountability
  • One person owns each key outcome end-to-end — no committees
  • Give owners the authority and budget to make real decisions
  • Track commitments, not just tasks — report on outcomes and blockers
Example: Every project launch has a single launch owner who coordinates all teams and makes the go/no-go call.

S
⭐
Standards
Set and hold the quality bar
  • Document what "good" looks like for every key output type
  • Use lightweight processes: peer reviews, QA checklists, acceptance criteria
  • Prevent quality drift without building heavy bureaucracy
Example: Create a shared "definition of done" checklist that every deliverable must pass before sign-off.

E
🔥
Engagement
Keep people motivated and connected
  • Build psychological safety: make it safe to speak up early about problems
  • Connect daily work to impact — show how contributions move the mission
  • Regular 1:1s focused on growth, public wins in team meetings
Example: Weekly demos where teams show progress and celebrate learning, not just success.

Building a Culture
People Want

79%
of people leave because they feel unappreciated
40%
of employees leave in the first year — bored or held back
Ways to build belonging
  • Conduct 1:1s when someone new joins
  • Don't only call members when it's about criticism
  • Always give shoutouts — prevent unseen efforts
  • Team hangouts, walks, lunches outside work context
  • Have-get-to-know-me sessions (people are more than roles)
  • Set monthly personal life goals — know how to support each person
  • Do in-person meetings whenever possible
  • Under promise, overdeliver — always
Culture isn't what you say.
It's what you tolerate.

Conflict Resolution
& Hard Conversations

✕   Never Do This
  • Make decisions without involving impacted members
  • Announce promotions or hires without team communication
  • Leave delegation unclear (who assigns work, who reports to whom)
  • Shift roles and responsibilities without warning
  • Ignore repeated negative signals — multiple small issues destroy teams
  • Wait to give feedback — address it on the spot
✓   Always Do This
  • Be proactive, not reactive — trust your gut if something feels off
  • Address the elephant in the room immediately (say the quiet part out loud)
  • Document boundaries — verbal is not enough, people forget
  • Watch if the same name keeps coming up in complaints
  • Diffuse small issues before they compound
  • Give feedback 1:1, never publicly humiliate
For you (the leader)
  • You have permission to feel angry, sad, or disappointed
  • Take ownership first — start with what you could have done better
  • Leading with ownership (not blame) lets the other person reflect
  • Have activities to re-establish presence if you've been distant
  • Prepare your lines in advance. Do not shrink or sugar coat.
For them (preserving dignity)
  • Have a concrete plan for offloading their work before you meet
  • Close the circle completely — zero ambiguity about their exit
  • Be honest and human — they deserve to understand why
  • If the conversation ends in yelling, they didn't feel heard
  • Ask yourself: "How can I help them?" even while acting decisively
Remember: If you feel relief after the confrontation, you waited too long. And being a leader is tiring — that's why you have a team. Do not do it alone.

Mental Health
& Wellbeing

💚
Emotional Health
How you manage feelings like stress, sadness, and joy — and how you help your team do the same.
🧠
Psychological Health
How you think, focus, solve problems, and understand yourself deeply enough to lead others well.
🤝
Social Health
How you build and maintain real relationships — and create a team that feels like a community, not just a crew.
Daily Practice
Do one healthy activity per day to boost your nervous system — how you think, feel, move, and react.
  • → Walking
  • → Cycling
  • → Swimming
  • → Yoga
  • → Hiking
  • → Box Breaths
  • → Relaxing in the sun
Weekly Reflection Questions
  • 01When was the last time you did a relaxation activity? How long did you feel good afterward?
  • 02Most EffectiveWhen was the last time you did something personally fulfilling — that aligns with your values?
  • 03What can you do to better your mental health — and why aren't you already taking action? (Remove judgment. Be curious.)
12%
of entrepreneurs are both healthy and successful.
What the 12% do differently
  • → Accept where they are — no resistance to reality
  • → Stay present — not living in past regret or future anxiety
  • → Practice patience — results compound over time
  • → Move their bodies — daily physical activity is non-negotiable
  • → Build communities around them — they don't do it alone
Entrepreneurship is no different from being an elite athlete.
The highest performers maximize their recovery so they can return to the next session fully charged. Productivity goes up when you give yourself space to slow down. Less is more. Do 30 minutes of daily self-reflection — if you don't know yourself, you can't lead others.

Why a Positive Mindset
Fuels Success

3×
more intelligence and creativity shown by doctors put in a positive mood before diagnosis
19%
faster accurate diagnoses made by doctors in a positive state vs. neutral
56%
more sales closed by optimistic salespeople over their pessimistic counterparts
Our brains are hardwired to perform at their best not when they are negative or neutral — but when they are positive. Yet in today's world, we sacrifice happiness for success, which ironically lowers our brain's ability to succeed.
The 7 Principles from "The Happiness Advantage"
01
The Happiness Advantage
Positive brains have a biological advantage over neutral or negative ones. Retrain your brain to capitalize on positivity and watch your productivity and performance follow.
02
The Fulcrum & the Lever
Your mindset is the fulcrum. How you position it determines how much power — the lever — you have to be fulfilled and successful. Adjust your mindset, and the world shifts with it.
03
The Tetris Effect
When your brain gets stuck scanning for stress and failure, you set yourself up to find exactly that. Train it instead to spot patterns of possibility — and you'll start seeing opportunities everywhere.
04
Falling Up
In defeat, stress, and crisis, your brain maps multiple paths forward. Find the one that doesn't just help you cope — but makes you happier and more successful because of the struggle.
05
The Zorro Circle
When challenges feel overwhelming, emotions hijack rational thinking. Regain control by starting small — focus on one manageable goal, then gradually expand your circle outward to bigger wins.
06
The 20-Second Rule
Willpower is limited — when it runs out, you fall back on old habits. Make tiny energy adjustments that reroute the path of least resistance, replacing bad habits with good ones almost automatically.
07
Social Investment
When stressed, most people retreat inward. The most successful people do the opposite — they invest in friends, peers, and family to propel themselves forward. Your social network is one of the greatest predictors of success.
Your brain performs best when it's positive. That's not soft — that's science.
Stop sacrificing happiness in the pursuit of success. The research is clear: happiness comes first, then success follows.
